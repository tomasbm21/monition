Reading a creditor list

A bankruptcy filing names one company. The papers that follow it name the next fifty. How the public record shows new legal work early, walked through one illustrative case.

By Tomás Borja Madero6 min readExamples are illustrative1

When a company files for bankruptcy protection, the filing is news for a day. Partners in restructuring see it, someone forwards it, and a few firms already acting for the company or its lenders get to work. For almost everyone else, the filing is the least useful page in the case.

The useful pages come later, and quietly. Over the following weeks the docket fills with motions, schedules and lists, and each one names companies that were not in the headline. Some of them will need counsel within months. Most of them do not know it yet.

This post walks through one case from the fictional dataset we use for every example on this site. The firm, the companies and the record numbers are invented. The shape of the case is not: it is how these records tend to arrive.

Day 0: the filing

Garnet Grill Holdings, a restaurant chain, files for bankruptcy protection. The docket opens as BK-26-04417. On its own, that record tells you one thing: a chain with dozens of sites, leases, suppliers and lenders is now in a process where many of those relationships will be renegotiated, ended or fought over.

A business development team that stops here has a list of one. The interesting question is who sits around the chain, and which of them the case is about to reach.

Day 2 to Day 30: the names arrive

Two days in, the chain asks to reject its lease at Halverson Plaza, a retail centre. That one entry turns a landlord into a company with a rent claim, an empty unit and, as it turns out, a dispute coming. A week later a contractor, Arvel Industrial, records a lien on the same property for unpaid fit-out work. Now there are two companies with a problem, linked by one address.

On Day 14 the creditor list is filed: 214 unsecured trade creditors. This is the page we think is most under-read in practice. It names suppliers such as Corliss Produce, Stellan Linen Services and Coldbrook Refrigeration, each owed money by a customer that may never pay it in full. A creditor list is not a list of companies in trouble. It is a list of companies whose next quarter just changed.

By Day 30 there is a sale motion: Pruett Franchise Partners bids for 12 of the sites. That brings in a buyer, the franchisees who operate under the brand, and the landlords whose leases will be assigned or rejected depending on which sites are sold.

Three kinds of names

Read enough of these cases and the names on the record sort themselves into a few groups, each pointing to a different practice:

  • Landlords, whose leases are listed for rejection. Real estate work: rent claims, re-letting, and sometimes a dispute over who pays for the fit-out.
  • Suppliers and trade creditors, who are owed money. Restructuring and litigation work: proving the claim, and in some cases advising the supplier on its own position.
  • Lenders, lessors and buyers, who hold security, lease equipment or want the assets. Banking and corporate work: enforcement, recovery, and the purchase itself.

Each of these is a matter that already exists the moment the record names the company. A partner who reads the record can see it. Monition calls this identifying: a record shows the need, so the question is only who in the firm knows the company.

From a list to a forecast

Identification is the easy half. The harder and more useful half is the company no filing names as being in trouble yet.

Take Coldbrook Refrigeration. It appears on the creditor list, but only as one creditor among 214. Read further back and further around, and other records line up. Its annual accounts from February note that one customer group made up most of its installation revenue. That customer is the chain that just filed. On Day 26 its own lender registers new security over its receivables, which usually means tighter terms. On Day 33 its next accounts are three weeks overdue at the companies register.

No single record says Coldbrook needs advice. Five records, read together, say it probably will, and roughly when.

That is a forecast: a company, a likely matter (refinancing or restructuring advice), a practice, and a timing range of two to four months, with the five records cited. It is made before any filing names Coldbrook as the company with the problem. If the forecast is right, the record will eventually confirm it, through a missed payment to the lender, a petition, or a sale of the business. If it is wrong, the record will show that too.

What we leave out

A creditor list can include people as well as companies: individual employees owed wages, sole traders, consumers with deposits. Monition reads about organisations only. Individuals, consumer debtors and sole traders are excluded before anything is forecast, and nothing about them reaches a partner.

The same list can include the firm’s own clients. In this case, Brightwater Capital holds security over the chain’s kitchen equipment, and Brightwater is a client of the fictional firm. Monition treats that as an alert to the relationship partner, not as a company to approach, and does not suggest approaching anyone who may be adverse to Brightwater.

What a creditor list does not tell you

It does not tell you whether a company has counsel already, whether it wants a new firm, or whether the firm should act. Those are judgements for a partner, after conflicts have been cleared in the firm’s own system. What the record can do is make sure the partner hears in time to make that judgement, instead of hearing after the company has already chosen.

Every case on the docket produces pages like these. The work is in reading all of them, every day, and noticing when five quiet records about one company start pointing the same way. That is the reading Monition does.
